> Production note: warm, unhurried. This is the only episode that talks about
> the series rather than about hardware. Keep it short.

## Cold open

You already think sequentially. Years of doing this have wired it in. You read a
problem, you break it into steps, you put the steps in order, and you walk them.
That habit is not a weakness and this series is not going to ask you to give it
up.

But for the next couple of hours I am going to ask you to set it down. Just for a
while. Because the first thing hardware does to a careful sequential thinker is
take the sequence away, and if you are holding on to it while that happens, the
whole subject feels like nonsense.

Then — and this is the part nobody tells you — we build the sequence back.
Deliberately, with our own hands, out of parts. And when you get it back, you
own it in a way you never did before, because you will have built the thing that
makes order out of simultaneity.

That is the arc. Lose the sequence, rebuild the sequence. Twelve episodes.

## Where we are

Nowhere yet. This is the beginning.

## What this series is

Twelve episodes, a bit over ten minutes each. About two and a half hours of listening.
Every episode does the same five things in the same order, because you are going
to hear them while driving and a predictable shape is a kindness.

First, where we are. A minute of recap. You heard the last one yesterday, in
traffic, and you were also merging.

Second, the problem. Something concrete that does not work yet. Not a definition
— a situation.

Third, the turn. One idea. Exactly one. The idea that makes the broken thing
work.

Fourth, the cost. Every idea in hardware takes something away from you. Faster
costs power. Smaller costs speed. Flexible costs area. The engineers who look
like wizards are just the ones who can name the cost out loud before they are
asked.

Fifth, the one thing. A single sentence. If you fall asleep in the middle, or a
lorry cuts you up and you lose two minutes, the one thing is what you keep.

Then I give you a question to think about on the way back. Nothing to write
down. All of them fit in your head.

## What this series is not

It is not a course in a language. I am not going to teach you Verilog or VHDL
syntax, because syntax is what you learn in an hour at a keyboard, and you
cannot practise typing while driving anyway.

It is not a tour of tools. No vendor names, no menus, no "click the third icon".

It is not a list of facts to memorise. There is almost nothing to memorise here.
Twelve ideas, and each one is small enough to say in a sentence.

What it is: a model. A way of looking at a digital design that makes the right
answers feel obvious and the wrong ones feel uncomfortable before you can
explain why. That feeling — the discomfort that arrives before the explanation —
is the actual skill. Everything else is lookup.

## How to actually listen

Three suggestions, and then I will get out of the way.

One. Listen in order. I know. But episode five leans on episode three so hard
that it will just sound like words if you skip. If you must skip something, skip
this one, the one you are listening to now.

Two. Do not take notes. You are driving. There is nothing here that needs a
note. If an idea is worth keeping, it will still be there when you park, and if
it is not, a note would not have saved it.

Three. Use the return leg. The commute exercise at the end of each episode is
not homework and nobody is marking it. But hardware understanding happens in the
gap between hearing a thing and needing it, and your return leg is the best gap
you have got. Turn the audio off. Let the question sit there. That silence is
doing more work than another twenty minutes of me talking would.

## Why hardware, why now

One last thing before we start properly.

Software has spent fifty years making the machine look like a list of
instructions, and it has done that so well that most people who write programs
have never once had to think about the fact that underneath, there is no list.
There is a slab of silicon in which several hundred million things are all
happening simultaneously and continuously, and the list is an illusion that a
great deal of clever engineering maintains on your behalf.

When you move to hardware, that engineering is no longer on your behalf. It is
your job. You are on the other side of the illusion now, and you are one of the
people maintaining it.

That is a strange and very good place to stand. Everything above you starts
making sense in a new way. Why memory is slow. Why parallelism is hard. Why
adding cores stopped helping. Why a cache miss costs what it costs. You stop
reading those as facts about computers and start reading them as consequences of
physics and money, which is what they always were.

So that is the real promise of the next two and a half hours. Not that you will
build something — you will need a keyboard for that. But that you will be able
to *see* it. And once you can see it, the building is mostly typing.

## The one thing

You are about to lose your sense of sequence, and then build it back yourself
out of parts. Both halves matter. Do not quit in the middle.

## Commute exercise

On the way home, pick any piece of electronics within arm's reach. Phone,
keyboard, the car's dashboard.

Ask yourself one question about it: how many things inside that device are
happening *right now*, at this instant, at the same time as each other?

Do not try to be accurate. There is no right number. Just notice which way your
instinct goes — do you reach for a small number, like four or eight, or a huge
one? And notice how hard your mind fights to line them up into an order anyway.

That fight is the thing we start dismantling tomorrow.
